Why Is Canada So Different From America?

One set of British colonists chose revolution, another chose loyalty, and each built a country in the image of that choice

Dominion Loyalist · November 2026

Watch the animated version on YouTube (30 minutes). The essay below follows the same path in writing.

We share the longest border in the world. We speak the same language, watch the same shows, and buy most of the same things.

And yet nobody who has spent a week in both countries thinks they are the same place. The two countries are far more alike than, say, England and China, but Canada and America are only similar on the surface. Scratch the skin, and the differences start to become obvious.

So why is Canada so different from America? The answer starts with a family, a war, and a choice made in 1783.

I. Two Children of the Same House

Start with something people forget. America was born British.

The thirteen colonies were settled by the English, the Scots and the Scots-Irish. They spoke English, read English law, and worshipped in English churches. In 1760 the men in Boston and the men in Halifax were subjects of the same king, and most of them were proud of it.

Then came the quarrel. Taxes, troops, representation in parliament, and the question of who gets to rule. By 1775 it was a war, and by 1776 it was a revolution. The rebels did not just want new management. They wanted a new kind of country: no king, no lords, no established church. Government by the consent of the governed, written down on paper.

Not everyone agreed. Somewhere between a fifth and a third of the colonists stayed loyal to the Crown. Some were officials and merchants. Many were ordinary farmers, tradesmen and tenants. They were called Tories by their neighbours, and many of them were literally beaten, tarred and feathered, and stripped of their land.

When the war ended in 1783, around sixty thousand of them left. Historian Maya Jasanoff counts about thirty-three thousand going to Nova Scotia, fourteen thousand of those to what became New Brunswick. Another six or seven thousand went up the St. Lawrence into what would become Ontario. With them came more than three thousand Black Loyalists who had been promised freedom for fighting for the King, and the Mohawk and other Haudenosaunee allies who settled along the Grand River and the Bay of Quinte.

Think about what that means. The founding population of English Canada was, to a large degree, made up of people who had looked at the American Revolution up close and said no.

That is the heart of it. The United States is the country of the revolution. English Canada is the country of the people who rejected it but were badly outnumbered.

The American sociologist Seymour Martin Lipset spent most of his career on this idea. He called the United States a country organized around a creed, liberty, equality and individualism, and Canada a country organized around continuity: the Crown, the law, the established order. As he put it, America came out of revolution and Canada out of counter-revolution.

The War of 1812 hardened it. American armies invaded Upper Canada, burned York (now Toronto), and expected to be welcomed. They assumed the Loyalists were just timid revolutionaries, not yet convinced to join the rebellion against the King. They were wrong, and the British colonists of Canada fought back with ferocity. The Americans burned Toronto, but the Empire captured Detroit and burned Washington. Out of that war came the Family Compact: the British, Tory, Anglican establishment of Upper Canada, built around men like Bishop John Strachan and Chief Justice John Beverley Robinson. About half of them were the children of Loyalists. Strachan had watched American troops loot his own house. He spent the rest of his life building schools, a church and a university meant to keep republicanism, which the Tories considered little better than mob rule, out of the province.

The historian Robert Passfield, who spent thirty years at Parks Canada, wrote a seven-hundred-page study of these men called The Upper Canadian Anglican Tory Mind. His argument is that they were not just a clique protecting their jobs. They were real philosophical conservatives, heirs of Richard Hooker and the balanced British constitution, and they were the only people in the province with a full vision of what it should become: a Christian, monarchical society, deliberately different from the republic across the river. His subtitle calls it a cultural fragment. A piece of old Britain, broken off and planted in the forests, that grew into something of its own.

We should be fair here. The Family Compact was also a closed, self-dealing elite, and by 1837 people were taking up arms against it. And the Loyalist story is partly a myth: by 1812, most people in Upper Canada were recent American settlers who came for cheap land, not for the King. The Tories were constantly nervous about that, and after the war it led to the settlement of many discharged British soldiers in the province to offset them. But myths that a country believes about itself still shape it. And this one shaped us.

Now look at what each country did with the rest of the world.

The United States became the great melting pot. Come from Ireland, Germany, Italy, Sweden, Russia, or Poland, swear to the creed, and in a generation your children are simply American. That promise was never extended equally. America barred Chinese immigrants in 1882 and set racial quotas in 1924. But the idea was there from the start: America is a country you can join by believing in it.

Canada did not work that way. Until 1947, there was no such thing as a Canadian citizen in law. People born here were British subjects. Immigration policy openly preferred the British, then northern Europeans, drawing immigrants from the same 800 km circle around Manchester that Canada had always drawn from. Ottawa, pressed by British Columbia, feared the West Coast would be swamped by Chinese labour. It charged Chinese immigrants a head tax from 1885 and shut them out almost completely in 1923. The arguments at the time were openly about race as much as numbers. Much the same was done to immigrants from India, through the continuous journey rule that turned back the Komagata Maru in 1914. The doors did not fully open to the world until new rules in 1962 and the points system in 1967. You did not become Canadian by signing on to an idea. You became a subject of the Crown, and the Crown was British.

That loyalty ran deep, and it was not free. Canada sent men to South Africa in 1899. In the First World War, a country of under eight million put more than six hundred thousand people in uniform and lost about sixty thousand of them. The fight over conscription in 1917 nearly split English and French Canada for good, two peoples who were blood-cousins, sharing kings and ancestry since the Normans crossed the Channel in 1066.

Rudyard Kipling summed up the relationship in 1897, after Canada gave British goods a preferential tariff. He wrote Canada as a daughter speaking to her mother: “Daughter am I in my mother’s house, but mistress in my own. Canada was called both the eldest son and the eldest daughter of the Empire, and for decades it tried to live up to the label.

America left home by slamming the door and shaking its fist through the window. Canada cringed, stayed, grew up, and took over the running of its own house one room at a time. When America came back and assumed Canada would join, it was Canada’s turn to slam the door. That difference in method, revolution against evolution, is the next piece of the story.

II. The King Who Never Rules

The Declaration of Independence promises life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness. Canada’s founding law, the British North America Act of 1867, gives Parliament the power to make laws for the “peace, order and good government” of Canada.

One is a list of rights held by the individual. The other is a job description for the state. That is not an accident.

Look at how each country got its freedom. The Americans fought a war for it. Canadians mostly asked politely and patiently. There were rebellions in Upper and Lower Canada in 1837, and both failed within weeks. What followed was Lord Durham’s report, and then, by 1848, responsible government in Nova Scotia and the Province of Canada: the Governor would act on the advice of ministers who held the confidence of the elected assembly. No war, no new constitution needed. The same institutions, with the same power quietly moved from London to the local legislature.

The pattern repeated for a century. Confederation in 1867. Control of foreign policy after the First World War. The Statute of Westminster in 1931. Canadian citizenship in 1947. Bringing the constitution home in 1982 (which is still argued about today, whether it was a good move or a bad move). Each step was a negotiation, not a revolt.

You can see the same instinct on the frontier. The American West was settled first and policed later: wagon trains, gold rushes, the cavalry, and the six-shooter. In Canada the order ran the other way. In 1874, before most settlers arrived, about two hundred and seventy-five men of the new North-West Mounted Police rode west from Manitoba to shut down the American whisky traders and plant the Crown’s law on the prairies.

We should not romanticize that. The same Crown later starved, confined and reeducated the First Nations of the plains, through the pass system and the residential schools. Disease and the collapse of the bison caused enormous suffering, and frontier settlers suffered mightily too. But Ottawa also used food rations as leverage to push the plains nations onto reserves, and the residential schools were policy, not disease. Not every official or every case was the same, but the poor treatment of the First Nations should not be overlooked. Order was not the same thing as justice. But law did arrive before the settlers, and that left a mark on how Canadians think about authority.

Now to the institution at the centre of all this: the Crown.

In law, the King of Canada holds enormous power. He appoints the Prime Minister. He summons and dissolves Parliament. Nothing becomes law without royal assent. He is supreme commander-in-chief of the military.

And since 1947, almost all of that power can be exercised by one person in Ottawa. That year, King George VI issued Letters Patent authorizing the Governor General “to exercise all powers and authorities lawfully belonging to Us in respect of Canada. For practical purposes, the Governor General is the King in Canada: the one who signs, swears in, dissolves and commands.

Here is the remarkable part. Those powers are real, exist to this day, and they almost never get used.

The last time a Governor General openly refused a Prime Minister’s advice was 1926, when Lord Byng refused Mackenzie-King’s request for an election. That was his right: the Conservatives held the most seats in the House, so Byng invited them to try to form a government instead of calling an expensive election so soon after the last one. Mackenzie-King turned the refusal into a campaign issue about Canadian independence, won, and no Governor General has publicly said no since. In 2008, Prime Minister Stephen Harper asked Michaëlle Jean to prorogue Parliament to avoid a confidence vote he was going to lose to a contentious Liberal and NDP coalition, one that could only govern with the backing of the separatist Bloc Québécois. She took about two hours to think about it, and said yes.

The reserve powers still exist on paper. In a true crisis, a Governor General could refuse a dissolution, or dismiss a Prime Minister who refused to leave after losing an election. But the office has spent a century proving it will not reach for power it does not need.

Now look south. The American president was meant to be the opposite of a king. Congress was written first in the Constitution, and the president was hemmed in by checks and balances.

Over two centuries, the office has grown in one direction. Franklin Roosevelt issued more than three thousand seven hundred executive orders. In 1973, the historian Arthur Schlesinger wrote a book called The Imperial Presidency about Nixon. In 2025, Donald Trump signed more than two hundred executive orders in a single year, more than in his whole first term. Wars are fought without declarations from Congress. Tariffs have been set by presidential order.

To be fair, the checks still work, sometimes. In February of 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled six to three that the president could not use emergency powers to impose tariffs, because taxing power belongs to Congress. And this growth did not belong to one party. Democrats and Republicans have both expanded the office whenever they held it.

But look at the strange result. The country that rejected a king has built a presidency with more personal power every decade. The country that kept the king has turned the Crown’s representative into a guardian who almost never acts.

There is a catch on our side too. Power that leaves the Crown does not disappear. In Canada much of it has flowed into the Prime Minister’s Office, where a leader with a majority can control Cabinet, caucus and Parliament with very few checks. That is a story for another video, a full one on the Crown in Canada. For now, the point is simple. Americans built their country around a written list of rights and a powerful executive. Canadians built theirs around an old institution, slowly emptied of personal power and filled with convention.

III. Big Country, Small Market

Ideas shape a country. So does a map.

The United States sits on a compact block of temperate land, laced with navigable rivers, with the Mississippi running down the middle like a spine. Its people spread out across it and built one enormous internal market.

Canada got the second-largest country on Earth, and most of it is rock, forest, muskeg and tundra. Most Canadians live in a thin ribbon along the southern edge, a few hundred kilometres from the border. Our natural trade routes run north and south. Every effort to tie the country together east to west has had to fight geography.

That is a big reason Canada leaned on the state. When the market would not build something, government did. The Canadian Pacific was built with huge public grants of cash and land. The Canadian National Railway was stitched together from bankrupt lines and nationalized. Then came Trans-Canada Air Lines, the CBC, Ontario Hydro, and during the Second World War, C.D. Howe’s twenty-eight Crown corporations. Lipset saw this as the counter-revolutionary instinct again: Americans distrust the state, Canadians expect the Crown to act.

The same small market produced something else: concentration. A country of a few million spread over thousands of kilometres can only support a few big players in many industries. So we got a handful of chartered banks, a few telecom companies, two major airlines, and a few grocery chains. Each is stable and well regulated, and none of them has much reason to take risks. Ottawa protected them, in part because a small country was afraid of having its few champions bought by Americans.

That stability has paid off. When the American banking system came apart in 2008, not one major Canadian bank failed or needed a bailout. That is a real achievement, and it comes from the same cautious culture.

But caution has a price, and the people who pay it are the ones who want to build something.

Let’s be clear about one thing. Canada does not lack entrepreneurs. We never have. The Loyalists cleared the forests of New Brunswick and Upper Canada with hand tools. Canadians gave the world insulin, the snowmobile, the Canadarm, and many other inventions. Shopify, BlackBerry, Nortel and Bombardier were all built here. Nortel is the cautionary tale: for years its networks were hacked, allegedly from China, and after it collapsed in 2009, many believe its know-how ended up powering Huawei. The technology came from here, and it ended up somewhere else.

The problem is what happens after the start. Venture capital here is smaller and more cautious. Regulators and incumbents make it hard for newcomers to grow. And a successful founder has a short drive to a market ten times the size, with more money, lower taxes on success, and bigger customers. So a great many of them go. Year after year, the founders, engineers, and doctors we trained head south, and the companies they build are American.

There is a cultural piece here too, and this one is my own opinion. Australians call it tall poppy syndrome: the urge to cut down whoever grows above the rest. Canadians are very good at it. We admire modesty, distrust ambition, and treat a loud success with suspicion. A country can do that and stay pleasant. It is much harder to do that and stay prosperous.

So here is the economic difference in one line. Americans built a country that rewards the person who takes a risk and wins. Canada built a country that protects people from losing. Both are choices, and both have costs. But for most of the last half century, the costs of ours have been showing up in the form of the people and companies that leave.

IV. The Family Falls Apart

In 1945 Canada was at its peak. It had the fourth-largest air force and one of the largest navies in the world. It had fought through Normandy, Italy and the Scheldt. For the Canadians who came home, being British and being Canadian were not in conflict. You were both, and proud of both, though aware of the differences between the colonial home and the mother islands.

Within twenty years, that world was gone.

The mother country was broke. Britain came out of the war exhausted, in debt, and shedding its empire. In 1956 Britain and France invaded Egypt over the Suez Canal, and Canada did not back them. Lester Pearson helped design the United Nations force that let Britain back out, and won the Nobel Peace Prize for it. In much of English Canada, it felt like the eldest child refusing to stand with its mother.

Britain, for its part, was turning away. In 1962 it restricted immigration from the Commonwealth. It applied to join the European Common Market, and on January 1, 1973, it joined, ending the system of imperial preference that had tied Canadian farms and factories to British buyers since 1897. On that same day, Britain’s new Immigration Act took effect, and most Canadians, without a British-born parent or grandparent, were now subject to immigration control in the mother country, while the French and Germans walked through as Europeans. To many Canadians it felt like being told to return the key to your parents’ house. Canada had sent more than a million men and women across the Atlantic in two wars, about one in ten Canadians in the Second World War alone. Now the old trading family was being wound up, and Britain did not put up much of a fight to keep it.

Meanwhile the new power was right next door. By the 1960s, Americans owned huge parts of Canadian industry. American culture filled our television and our radio. And in 1963, John Diefenbaker’s government fell after a bitter fight with Washington over putting nuclear warheads on American-designed Bomarc missiles in Canada.

The philosopher George Grant wrote a short, angry book about it in 1965: Lament for a Nation. His argument was that Canada had only ever existed as a conservative, British alternative to America, and with that alternative gone, the country could not survive as anything but a branch of the American empire.

So picture the situation. The eldest child of a family that had broken up. The parent gone off to make a new life with European strangers. Living next to the rebel cousin who had become the richest and most powerful man on the street. And at home, a second crisis: Quebec’s Quiet Revolution, a new nationalism there, and by 1970, bombs and the October Crisis, from our own ancient blood-cousins.

Ottawa’s answer was to build a new identity, fast.

The Maple Leaf flag came first, after six months of bitter debate and some nasty political tricks. In committee, the Conservatives voted for the single red maple leaf, expecting the Liberals to back Pearson’s own three-leaf design, the “Pearson Pennant. The Liberals voted for the single leaf too, and the result was fourteen to nothing. Closure ended the debate in the House, the proud Red Ensign came down, and the Maple Leaf was raised on Parliament Hill on February 15, 1965. The three armed services were merged into one in 1968, with one green uniform, and the word “Royal” was dropped. The Army, Navy and Air Force lost their names, their uniforms and much of their tradition, and thousands of men and women resigned in protest, including many high-ranking officers and senior enlisted. The Official Languages Act made Canada bilingual from coast to coast in 1969. In 1971, Pierre Trudeau declared a policy of multiculturalism within a bilingual framework. Royal Mail became Canada Post. O Canada became the official anthem in 1980. Dominion Day became Canada Day in 1982, the same year the Constitution came home. The British North America Act was renamed the Constitution Act, 1867, and came home with a Charter of Rights and an amending formula so demanding that it has proven nearly impossible to use for any major change.

It is worth asking why each of these happened, because the reasons were not crazy. The new flag was meant to belong to French and English Canadians alike, and a Red Ensign with a Union Jack in the corner was never going to win Quebec, despite carrying the fleur-de-lis in its shield. Maybe its meaning was never properly conveyed to the Québécois, or the shared blood between English and French Canadians was never properly explained. Who knows. Multiculturalism came largely out of pressure from Ukrainian Canadians and others on the prairies, who had built the West and did not like being left out of the “two founding peoples,” which may have been a reasonable concern despite their being a small fraction of the population. And Canada had to answer the honest question of what it was, if not a British dominion.

Some of it worked. The flag is definitely iconic and recognizable, though still not loved by everyone, particularly those of the old British stock. Ontario and Manitoba immediately adopted Red Ensigns of their own as provincial flags in protest, and fly them to this day. Quebec voted twice on leaving, in 1980 and in 1995, and stayed both times, the second time by about one percentage point. Canada has absorbed one of the largest immigrant populations in the developed world, with less backlash than most.

But look at what the method was. America invented its identity in a revolution, and it built that identity on something it had: a creed. Canada rebuilt its identity in about twenty years, mostly by removing things. The British things. The royal things. The old name. What replaced them was a new set of symbols, and a definition that was easy to state and hard to build on: Canada is not America.

V. What We Lost, and What’s Left

Go to a Legion hall or a cenotaph on the eleventh of November, and you will often see an old flag flying beside the Maple Leaf. Red, with the Union Jack in the corner and the shield of Canada in the fly. The Canadian Red Ensign.

It was the flag Canadian troops carried into Normandy. It flew over government buildings from 1945 until 1965. For veterans, it is the flag men died under, and the Legion still carries it in its colour parties.

It also turns up in other places. Some fly it simply out of love for the country their grandparents knew. Some fly it as a protest against the post-war order, a way of saying the country took a wrong turn in the sixties. And some nationalist groups have adopted it as a rebellious symbol, claiming Canada as theirs because their ancestors were the ones who built it. Plenty of Canadians would call that extreme. The people flying it would call it heritage.

We should be honest about all three. A flag can carry more than one meaning at once. The answer to people who misuse it is not to hand it over to them. It is to remember what it actually stood for: a country that was British in its institutions, built by Loyalists, French Canadians, Black Loyalists, First Nations allies, Scots, Irish and later Ukrainians and many others, all under one Crown.

So, are we really that different from the Americans? The honest answer is: less than we like to think, and more than they assume.

Sociologists Edward Grabb and James Curtis looked at decades of survey data and found that English Canadians and northern Americans hold very similar values. Most of the gap comes from two outliers: Quebec on one end, and the American South on the other. Pollster Michael Adams argued the opposite in Fire and Ice: that on authority, religion and family, Canadians and Americans have been drifting apart. The truth is probably that both are right about different things. In daily life, we are close cousins. In our institutions and our instincts about the state, we are not.

Here is what I think we lost.

We did not lose our differences. We lost the story of why we have them. A generation of Canadians was raised to think of the country as having started in 1965, or 1982, and to treat everything before that as a little embarrassing. In 2015, then-Prime Minister Justin Trudeau told the New York Times that Canada has no core identity and no mainstream, and called it the first post-national state. He meant it as praise. That same year he had campaigned against Stephen Harper for using the phrase “old stock Canadians. For a great many of those old stock Canadians, “no core identity” was not praise at all. It was a grave insult. When a country cannot say what it is, it ends up describing itself only by what it is not, and “not American” is a thin thing to build on when the Americans come asking for a new deal.

We also lost confidence. The Loyalists were not timid people. They built two new provinces out of the bush and the wilds. It was a Tory government that built a railway across the continent, and C.D. Howe, a Liberal, who built twenty-eight Crown corporations in a few years. The old Canada was cautious about revolution, but it was not cautious about building. Somewhere along the way, we kept the caution and dropped the ambition. It didn’t matter if it was the Conservatives or the Liberals leading the government at the time, they governed with the same overall strategic values, until 1965.

And here is what we kept.

We kept the Crown, and with it a head of state who sits above party, and a system that still runs on convention and restraint. We kept peace, order and good government, and a deep public trust that the law will arrive before the settlers do. We kept a federation that has held together through two referendums. We kept a country you can actually join, now open to the whole world. In 2011, the Navy and the Air Force got back the word “Royal. Small things, but a sign that the older story is not as dead as George Grant thought.

So why is Canada so different from America? Because two hundred and forty years ago, one set of British colonists chose revolution, and another chose loyalty, and each built a country in the image of that choice. America became a creed. Canada became an inheritance.

America became a creed. Canada became an inheritance.

The question for us is whether we still know what we inherited, and whether we’re willing to build on it again.
