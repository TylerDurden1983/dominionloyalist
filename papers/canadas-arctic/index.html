Canada Can’t Defend Its Own Arctic

Ottawa finally has a plan for the North. Here is what it buys, and what is still missing.

Dominion Loyalist · November 2026

Watch the animated version on YouTube (8 minutes). The essay below follows the same path in writing.

In 2007, Ottawa promised the navy a refuelling station at Nanisivik, on the northern tip of Baffin Island, at the eastern mouth of the Northwest Passage. It was supposed to be running by 2015.

In 2018, the Auditor General warned that it would be usable only about four weeks a year.

In May 2026, National Defence shut it down. About $110 million had been spent, and another $200 million would have been needed to finish it. It never supported routine naval operations.

That has been Canada’s Arctic policy for a generation: a big announcement, a long wait, and a quiet exit. This year was supposed to be different. Ottawa has announced a northern plan worth roughly $40 billion: airfields, radar, roads, a port and submarines. So is Canada finally securing its Arctic, or is this Nanisivik with more zeros?

I. The Empty North

Start with the scale. The three territories are about 40 percent of Canada’s land. About 135,000 people live there, a mid-sized Ontario city spread across an area bigger than India.

Now the military. As recently as 2023, National Defence counted about 300 full-time personnel in the entire North. Alongside them are about 2,000 Canadian Rangers: part-time reservists, most of them Indigenous, who patrol with a rifle and a lifetime of knowing the land.

The hardware was just as thin: a signals station at Alert on the top of Ellesmere Island, a headquarters in Yellowknife, a training centre in Resolute, and a chain of radars from the 1980s.

Across the pole, Russia runs the largest icebreaker fleet on earth, about eight nuclear-powered ships and thirty-four diesel-electric by one U.S. intelligence count. It has reopened Soviet-era bases along its Arctic coast, and about two-thirds of its sea-based nuclear deterrent sits on the Kola Peninsula. To be fair, American intelligence says the war in Ukraine is draining Russia’s Arctic plans. But the bases are still there.

China calls itself a “near-Arctic state.” Its nearest point is about 1,400 kilometres south of the Arctic Circle. But every summer its research icebreakers come north, and Canadian aircraft shadow them. Mapping the sea floor is science. It is also exactly what a submarine fleet needs.

And then there is our closest ally. The United States has never accepted that the Northwest Passage is Canadian water. Washington calls it an international strait.

Here is the part that gets left out. Sovereignty is not a colour on a map. It comes down to who is actually there, and for thousands of years the people actually there have been Inuit. Canada’s claim to those waters leans heavily on Inuit use and occupancy. Any plan that treats the North as empty space is wrong on the history and weak on the law.

The North is not empty. It is undefended. Those are different problems.

II. Ottawa Finally Moves

On March 12, 2026, in Yellowknife, the Prime Minister announced the largest northern build since the Cold War radar lines. Credit where it is due. Here is what is in it.

Airfields. $32 billion to rebuild NORAD’s northern bases at Yellowknife, Inuvik, Iqaluit and Goose Bay: longer runways, hangars, fuel and ammunition storage.

Support hubs. Yellowknife, Inuvik and Iqaluit were chosen in 2025. The plan adds Whitehorse and Resolute, with smaller nodes at Cambridge Bay and Rankin Inlet: airstrips, warehouses and supplies already in place, so a response starts in the North instead of in Trenton, about 3,500 kilometres from Resolute.

Roads. Today the only road to the Arctic coast ends at Tuktoyaktuk, and its harbour is too shallow for most ships. So the plan goes east: a 400-kilometre all-season road from the Yellowknife region to the Nunavut border, then 230 more to a new deepwater port at Grays Bay. It would be Canada’s first deepwater port on the Arctic Ocean, and Nunavut’s first road to the rest of the country.

Radar. A $2.5 billion deal with Australia for over-the-horizon radar that can see far past the old radar chain.

Submarines. Up to twelve, from Germany’s TKMS, which would be the largest defence purchase in Canadian history.

That is a real plan, more than any government has put forward in a generation. Now the fine print. The radar’s first capability is targeted for the end of 2029, and full operation for 2043. The roads and the port are aimed at the early 2030s. The submarine contract is still being negotiated. Every one of these is a promise with a date on it. So was Nanisivik.

III. What’s Still Missing

Even if every piece gets built, there are three holes in the map.

Churchill

Churchill, Manitoba is the only deepwater port in the Canadian Arctic with a railway to the south. The Hudson’s Bay Company set up there in 1717, with wooden ships and no weather forecast. A military base stood there from 1942 until it was torn down in 1981. And in 1997, Ottawa sold the port to an American company for ten dollars.

By 2016 the port was closed. In 2017 a flood cut the rail line. Today it is owned by northern and Indigenous communities, and it is working again. In August 2026, about 30,000 tonnes of prairie durum left Churchill for Europe, the first grain through the port since 2020. Zinc and potash followed. Ottawa and the provinces have committed about $262 million.

But the full build, an upgraded railway, an all-season road and an energy corridor, would cost billions and is not funded. And in Ottawa’s northern defence plan, Churchill does not appear at all. No navy, no army, no air force. The one Arctic port we can already reach by rail has no military role.

In fairness, Hudson Bay is iced over about eight months of the year, and the track runs across muskeg and permafrost. Those are real engineering problems. They are also the kind a serious country solves.

The eastern gate

Grays Bay covers the west. In the east, Nanisivik was the plan, and Nanisivik is gone. The nearest deepwater port is Iqaluit, more than a thousand kilometres south of the passage. Pond Inlet sits near the entrance. A modest dock and a fuel depot there would do what Nanisivik never did. Yes, the season is short and the cost is high. That was true in 2007 as well. The need has not moved.

People

A hub is a runway, a warehouse and a stockpile. That is useful, but it is somewhere forces pass through, not somewhere they live, and the plan names no permanent garrisons.

Meanwhile, as Ottawa talks about a build-up, the army in November 2026 began cancelling reserve training and cutting reserve jobs to pay for other priorities. National Defence’s own mobilization plan calls for a reserve of 100,000. You do not get there by cutting.

There is a case against garrisons. The Forces are short of people, and northern postings are expensive. But you cannot fly in local knowledge. A permanent Arctic battalion in Yellowknife, and a larger, better-equipped Ranger force, would give Canada soldiers who know the ground before the emergency starts. A base town brings housing, power and jobs that outlast any exercise, provided it is built the way Churchill and Grays Bay are being built: with northerners as owners, not as scenery.

The Verdict

So is this Nanisivik with more zeros? Not yet. For the first time in a generation the plan is big enough. What is unproven is the follow-through.

So do not judge it by the announcement. Judge it in 2035. Is there fuel in the tanks? Are there ships at the dock? Are there Canadians living there in uniform?

The Hudson’s Bay Company built Churchill in 1717 with sailing ships. We have no excuse.
