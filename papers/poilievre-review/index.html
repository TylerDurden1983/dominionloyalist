Poilievre’s 87% Had a $999 Cover Charge

The leadership review was a real vote. It was also a vote that was built to win. Holding the Leader of the Opposition to the same standard as the Prime Minister.

Dominion Loyalist · November 2026

Watch the animated version on YouTube (8 minutes). The essay below follows the same path in writing, with a little more detail.

In January 2026, Pierre Poilievre walked into a convention hall in Calgary and walked out with 87.4 percent approval. The headlines called it a landslide vote of confidence. The pundits said the question of his leadership was settled.

Since then, two more of his MPs have crossed the floor to the Liberals. His deputy chief of staff is leaving, the third senior aide to go in less than a year. And on December 7, his party is defending a seat it held in Brantford, with a candidate the leadership picked over the heads of local members, and a longtime local Conservative running against him as an independent.

A little while ago we took a hard look at Mark Carney and Brookfield. Fair is fair. This is the Leader of the Opposition held to the same standard, using the same yardstick: who gets a say, who has access, and what happens to the people who don’t do what they’re told.

So start with the number. 87.4 percent. Of whom?

I. A Career Inside the Game

Poilievre was first elected to Parliament in 2004, in the Ottawa-area riding of Nepean-Carleton, at 25. Before that he was a political staffer. Politics is the only career he has really had: 22 years in the House, 11 of them in opposition, the last four as Leader of the Opposition.

That is not a crime. Parliament needs people who know how it works, and almost nobody disputes that Poilievre is one of the sharpest operators in the House. He was Stephen Harper’s minister of democratic reform, then his employment minister, and in 2022 he won the party leadership on the first ballot with 68 percent.

But a career spent inside the game shapes how you play it.

In April 2025 he lost the federal election, and he lost his own seat in Carleton, which he had held for two decades. An Alberta Conservative MP, Damien Kurek, stepped aside, and Poilievre ran in Battle River–Crowfoot, about as safe a Conservative seat as exists in the country. He won it in August 2025. Under the party’s rules, a leader who loses an election and stays on faces a review. That review is where the 87.4 percent comes from.

II. Built to Win

The rule itself is fair. Under the Conservative constitution, a leader who loses and doesn’t resign faces a mandatory review at the next national convention. Harper went through one in 2005 and got 84 percent. Nobody should object to a party asking whether its leader still has its confidence.

What deserves a look is how the room was built.

Who could vote

Only delegates in the room could vote, not every member of the party. Each riding association could elect up to ten members at a local meeting, and the riding president and the MP or candidate went automatically. Then came the toll: the delegate fee was $999. That was before flights to Calgary, a hotel, meals and time off work.

The convention was in Calgary. In January. In the heart of the party’s base. On the same weekend as the Ontario PC convention, which, as Don Newman pointed out in Policy Magazine, kept a good number of the Ontario Conservatives most skeptical of Poilievre busy somewhere else. The federal party’s trouble understanding Ontario is a story of its own, and one we will come back to.

The Canadian Press reported that Poilievre’s team spent months identifying delegates and gauging their support. About 2,400 voting delegates registered. At roughly a thousand dollars a head, that is in the neighbourhood of $2.4 million in tickets, a little less once early-bird and youth rates are counted.

For scale

When Poilievre won the leadership in 2022, roughly 678,000 members were eligible to vote, and all it took was a party membership, about fifteen dollars. Not a thousand-dollar ticket and a trip to Calgary in winter.

There are 343 ridings in Canada. Two thousand four hundred delegates works out to about seven per riding, in theory. Which ridings actually sent how many, if any, is not something we could find published anywhere.

87.4 percent is a real number. It is 87.4 percent of a room that was expensive to get into, in the friendliest city the party could pick.

Outside the room, the picture was softer. Abacus found that 76 percent of Conservative voters wanted to keep him. Among all Canadians, it was 34 percent.

Conservatives spend a lot of airtime accusing the Liberals of playing politics. This was the party playing politics with itself. Nothing here broke the rules. The rules were the weapon.

III. The Caucus Kept Walking

Here is what that room did not capture. Since the 2025 election, eight MPs have left Poilievre’s caucus.

Some of that is ordinary. Cathay Wagantall had already said she was not running again. Larry Brock went back to his old job as a Crown prosecutor. Scott Aitchison, after 30 years in elected office, says it is time to try something else. Richard Martel took a Senate seat Carney offered him, and has since rejoined the Conservative group in the Senate. People leave. That happens in every caucus.

Then there are the other four. They did not retire. They crossed the floor and sat down with the Liberals.

Chris d’Entremont went in November 2025, and he named Poilievre’s leadership style as a factor. Michael Ma followed weeks later. Then came the 87.4 percent. After it, Edmonton MP Matt Jeneroux crossed in February 2026, and in April it was Marilyn Gladu of Sarnia, one of the most socially conservative MPs in the House. Three months earlier, she had said floor-crossers should have to face a by-election.

To be fair

Carney went fishing. His finance minister said openly that Conservative MPs would do some soul-searching over the holidays. And as Dominic LeBlanc pointed out, Conservatives cheered when a Liberal crossed to them under Harper. Floor-crossing is not new, and it is not one-sided.

But four in five months, from one caucus, is not normal. The convention said 87 percent. The caucus kept walking.

And the staff

It is not just MPs. His communications director left in the spring, and his chief of staff since 2022, Ian Todd, retired at the end of the spring sitting. This month his deputy chief of staff, Aaron Wudrick, announced he is leaving for the private sector, along with a handful of others in the leader’s office, including senior communications staff. By every account they left on good terms, some of them for family reasons.

People change jobs, and we would not read much into any one of these. Together, three weeks before two by-elections, they are worth noticing.

IV. Who Gets Access

Now the access question, the same one we asked about Brookfield.

Poilievre’s closest adviser is Jenni Byrne. She was Stephen Harper’s deputy chief of staff, she is widely described as Poilievre’s chief strategist, and she is his ex-girlfriend. She also runs a government relations firm in Toronto, Jenni Byrne and Associates.

In 2024, six of her firm’s lobbyists were registered to lobby the Ontario government for Loblaw. That is the same grocery giant Poilievre was hammering over food prices, and over the $12 million the Trudeau government gave it in 2019 for new fridges and freezers, as if Loblaws needed the money. Byrne herself was not listed, and her firm says she never lobbied federally for Loblaw.

Then the Globe and Mail found something else. On the first business day after Poilievre won the leadership in 2022, the president and senior vice-president of Byrne’s firm incorporated a second company, Forecheck Strategies. Same office, many of the same staff, lobbying at the federal level. NDP MPs and Democracy Watch asked the lobbying commissioner to investigate.

Is that Brookfield? No. Carney is Prime Minister and holds the levers; Byrne advises the opposition. But it is the same shape: the people closest to the leader, and the companies paying the people around them.

Brantford

On who gets a say inside the party, look at Brantford–Brant South. When Larry Brock resigned, the party skipped the local nomination and appointed the city’s mayor, Kevin Davis. The two former MPs for the area walked out of the rally where it was announced. Now a longtime local Conservative, pastor Dave Carroll, is running against him as an independent.

That by-election is December 7. Poilievre says he is staying on as leader whatever happens.

The Cartoon Choice

Online, the joke writes itself. Carney gets compared to Mr. Burns. Poilievre gets compared to Milhouse. Some days it feels like that is the permanent choice on the ballot.

On one side, Mr. Burns does Mr. Burns things: the money, the funds, the pension deals, all reaching back to the same tower. On the other, Milhouse runs his own party with a nervous iron fist: hand-picked candidates, an ex-girlfriend with a lobbying firm in the inner circle, a review where only the faithful could afford to vote, and MPs heading for the exits.

The honest ledger

Fair is fair here too. Poilievre broke no rule. The review was held exactly as the party constitution requires, the delegate system is how the party has long run its conventions, and a leader is entitled to organize his supporters. Most of the MPs who left had ordinary reasons. Byrne’s firm says she never lobbied for Loblaw federally, and no finding has been made against her. And a leader who loses an election and keeps his party at 87 percent in the room and 76 percent among its voters is not a leader without support.

But the same point we made about Carney applies: “within the rules” is a low bar when the people at the top write the rules. A party that wants to govern a country should be able to pass these:

1. One member, one vote on leadership reviews. The same membership that chose the leader should get to judge him, by mail or online, without a thousand-dollar ticket and a flight.

2. Publish the delegate count by riding. If 2,400 people decide a leader’s future, members should know where they came from.

3. Let riding associations choose their own candidates. Appointments should be the rare exception, explained in public, not the way a safe seat gets filled.

4. Keep the leader’s inner circle out of the lobbying business, or at least publish every client of every firm run by someone who advises the leader.

Canada deserves better than a choice between those two.

So the question isn’t which cartoon you like more. It’s why those are the only two on the ballot.
