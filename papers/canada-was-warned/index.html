Canada Was Warned About Immigration. It Did It Anyway.

How the country that invented the modern immigration system lost its people’s trust, who wanted it that way, and what it actually costs

Dominion Loyalist · November 2026

Watch the animated version on YouTube (11 minutes). The essay below follows the same path in writing, with a few points the video had no room for.

In 2022, Ottawa’s own immigration analysts sent a warning up to the deputy minister. Housing construction, they wrote, had not kept up with population growth. Rapid increases would put pressure on health care and affordable housing.

The targets went up anyway. The levels plan published that November set permanent resident targets of 465,000 for 2023, 485,000 for 2024 and 500,000 for 2025.

On November 5th, 2026, it happened again. Buried in the federal budget is a one-time pathway for up to 200,000 temporary residents already living here to become permanent residents by the end of 2027. They will not count against the annual immigration target.

So let’s add it all up, honestly. The 2027 target is 380,000 permanent residents. Add the new pathway and that is up to 580,000, an all-time record; the previous record, set in 2024, was 483,655. Add up to 148,000 more still coming through last year’s one-time programs for protected persons and work permit holders. Add the 370,000 new temporary workers and students the plan allows. And if asylum claims hold near the refugee board’s own expectation, add about 100,000 more.

All in: up to 1.2 million people admitted, made permanent, or claiming asylum, in a single year.

That figure needs a caveat, and it deserves one. It is an upper bound, because the one-time programs are spread across two years. And it is not population growth: many of these people are already here, and some will leave. But it is the honest total of what the plan does in one year, and it is not the number Canadians are usually told.

In 2024, for the first time in a quarter century, a clear majority of Canadians told the Environics Institute that the country takes in too many immigrants: 58 percent. So how did the country that invented the modern immigration system lose faith in it? Who wanted this? And what does it actually cost?

I. The Country That Chose

Until the 1960s, Canada picked immigrants largely by where they came from. The regulations openly preferred British subjects, Americans and the French, then northern and western Europeans. In 1967 that changed. Canada became the first country in the world to use a points system, scoring applicants on education, occupation, age and language rather than on origin.

It worked because of geography. Canada has three oceans and one land border, and the country across that border is richer than we are. So Ottawa could do something most countries cannot: set a number, choose people against it, and plan the country around it.

That does not mean Canadians were ever thrilled about immigration. They were not. In 1977, 61 percent told pollsters they wanted it cut. Through the 1980s and early 1990s Ottawa pushed annual intake from roughly 100,000 to more than 250,000, and through those years a clear majority said the level was too high.

Then, in the mid-1990s, Ottawa listened. It held permanent immigration at about 250,000 a year for two decades and shifted the mix hard toward skilled workers. Slowly, public opinion came around.

That was the deal. It was never written down, but everyone understood it. Keep the numbers steady. Pick people who will work. Make sure the homes, the hospitals and the roads keep up. Do that, and Canadians will put up with it, and in time come to like it.

One more feature of that era matters. Most of those newcomers were permanent residents from the day they landed. They were chosen, counted, and expected to stay. The system that broke the deal was the one that was never meant to be permanent at all.

II. The Back Doors

After 2015, the permanent target went up. That part, at least, was debated in public: it appears in a levels plan tabled in Parliament every year. The temporary side was not. International students, work permit holders, temporary foreign workers and asylum claimants had no annual cap at all. After the pandemic, Ottawa opened the taps. In 2023 Canada’s population grew 3.2 percent, the fastest rate since 1957, and nearly all of it came through the temporary streams.

Who wanted it

This did not happen by accident. The Century Initiative, a lobby for a Canada of 100 million people by 2100, was co-founded by Dominic Barton, then the global head of McKinsey, and Mark Wiseman, a senior BlackRock executive. Both sat on Ottawa’s Advisory Council on Economic Growth, which in 2016 recommended raising permanent immigration from 300,000 to 450,000 a year.

Business pressure went beyond the think tanks. Tim Hortons lobbied Ottawa for more than a year to ease the limits on temporary foreign workers. The restaurant, retail and small-business lobbies fought every restriction proposed on the program.

In April 2022 Ottawa gave them most of what they asked for. Under the Workforce Solutions Road Map, employers could fill up to 20 percent of their low-wage positions with temporary foreign workers, and up to 30 percent in seven sectors including food service and construction, up from 10 percent. The rule that automatically refused low-wage applications in regions where unemployment was 6 percent or higher was dropped. In 2017, about 1 in 14 food-service workers was a temporary resident. By 2023, it was 1 in 5.

The student door

The student door was worse. International tuition became a business model for colleges, and a study permit became a ticket to a work permit and, for many, a path to permanent residence. When Ottawa finally began verifying college acceptance letters in late 2023, it flagged more than 10,000 as fraudulent within about a year.

Asylum

Then there is asylum. In 2024, a record 190,039 claims were referred to the Immigration and Refugee Board. More than 20,000 came from international students, nearly double the year before and six times as many as in 2019. The immigration minister himself said many of those student claims appeared to be made “mostly falsely.”

Why would anyone file a false claim? Because the wait is the prize. By the end of 2025, about 300,000 claims were waiting for a decision, with waits approaching four years. During that time a claimant can live and, in most cases, work in Canada. Ottawa has spent more than a billion dollars on hotels for asylum seekers since 2017, and another one and a half billion helping provinces and cities house them.

To be fair, a genuine refugee from a warzone deserves protection, and Canada should give it. But look at what actually happens. Of all the claims the board closed in the first half of 2025, fewer than a third were accepted. The rest were rejected, abandoned or withdrawn, many after years in Canada. (Counting only the claims decided on their merits, acceptance runs much higher, which is exactly why the abandoned and withdrawn claims matter: they are the people who used the wait and never tested their case.)

Some claims are nearly impossible to test. Legal Aid Ontario once flagged that over a few months, 60 to 70 percent of Nigerian claims it handled cited sexual orientation. Claims on that ground have been accepted in Canada since the early 1990s, and rightly so, since people are persecuted for it. But since 2017 the board’s own guideline has told members to avoid stereotypes when judging such claims, which in practice makes them harder to challenge. And once a claim is accepted, a spouse and children can follow.

A system where a weak claim still buys you four years is a system that invites weak claims.

The economy

The economy did not boom on the extra people. GDP grew, because more people produce more output, but GDP per person fell for six quarters in a row. Youth unemployment hit 14.6 percent in 2025, a fifteen-year high outside the pandemic. The economist Mike Moffatt put the low-wage stream bluntly: even when it works as intended, it suppresses wages. “It’s kind of designed to.”

Here too, both columns. A federal evaluation of the temporary foreign worker program did not find wage suppression at the national level, though it did flag the risk in local labour markets and particular occupations. And when the program was tightened in 2014, wages in the affected jobs rose by roughly 4 percent. Both of those findings point the same way: in the jobs where the stream concentrates, it holds wages down.

Concentration

The change was also concentrated. In Brampton, 52.9 percent of residents were born outside Canada at the 2021 census. A handful of newcomers in a town is fine, even interesting; most Canadians have lived that and liked it. A town you no longer recognize after fifteen years is something else. Research on immigration attitudes across Europe and North America finds the same thing again and again: it is the speed of local change, more than the level, that drives backlash, while time and ordinary contact soften it.

That is the soil the new nationalist groups grew in, the ones marching under the Red Ensign. They are a symptom. Ignoring the cause will not make them go away.

III. The Honest Ledger

So does immigration pay? Start with the two arguments you always hear.

“We’re not having kids”

Canada’s fertility rate fell to 1.25 children per woman in 2024, a record low and far below the 2.1 needed to replace the population. But ask why. In Statistics Canada’s surveys, Canadian women say they want about 2.2 children. In 2022, 38 percent of Canadians in their twenties said they could not afford a child in the next three years.

Push down wages and push up rents, and you get fewer Canadian babies. Then the shortfall is used to justify more immigration, which pushes rents up again. That is not a solution to low fertility. It is a cycle.

In 2024, 42 percent of babies born in Canada had a foreign-born mother, up from about 22 percent in 1997. Some of those babies have Canadian-born fathers, and every one of them is a Canadian. But the number is striking either way, and a country that can no longer afford its own children should be concerned by it.

“We need the workers”

All of Canada’s labour force growth in the ten years to 2024 came from immigration. But being in the labour force is not the same as working. In 2024, unemployment among recent immigrants reached 12.6 percent, more than double the rate for the Canadian-born. Importing workers into a softening job market does not fill shortages. It creates competition for the jobs that exist.

Who actually does well

The data on social assistance, from IRCC’s own Longitudinal Immigration Database, surprises people on both sides of this debate. Ten years after landing, 2 percent of economic immigrants are on social assistance. The Canadian average is 8 percent. Immigrants overall sit at about 6 percent. The people Canada deliberately selects for skills do better than the average Canadian.

Refugees are a different story: 36 percent in their first year, partly because government-assisted refugees start on federal resettlement support, and still 25 percent a decade later. And asylum claimants are not in that data at all, because they have not landed yet; their support comes through provincial programs and federal housing money.

The front door works. The back doors are where the costs pile up.

What nobody says out loud

Integration happens over generations, and the surest sign of it is marriage. In 2021, more than four in ten Canadians were immigrants or the children of immigrants. In the City of Toronto, it was eight in ten. Only one Torontonian in five had two Canadian-born parents.

Small groups, spread out, blend in fast. The Dutch and the Danes who came a century ago are simply Canadians now. Large groups concentrated in a few places blend more slowly. In 2011, only 13 percent of South Asian couples and 19 percent of Chinese couples in Canada were mixed unions, against 79 percent of Japanese couples. Statistics Canada’s own explanation is group size: the bigger the community, the easier it is never to leave it. The number of neighbourhoods in which a single visible-minority group made up most of the residents rose from 6 in 1981 to 254 in 2001.

Fair caveat: time works. Among visible-minority Canadians, the share in mixed unions rises from 12 percent in the first generation to 51 percent in the second and 69 percent by the third. The Canadian integration engine is real, and it is one of the country’s great achievements. But it only works if the pace allows it. Britain and France are now living with what happens when it does not.

IV. What the Law Actually Requires

Let’s be plain about one thing. No law says Canada has to take in the world.

Canada’s binding obligations are narrow. Under the 1951 Refugee Convention, we cannot send a genuine refugee back to persecution. And since the Supreme Court’s Singh decision in 1985, anyone who claims asylum on Canadian soil is entitled to an oral hearing. Those are real duties, and Canada should honour them.

Everything else is a choice. Every worker, every student, every permanent resident admitted is admitted because a government decided to admit them. The number is not imposed on us by treaty or by the courts. It is set in Ottawa, and it can be set differently.

And the current system is not fair to the newcomers either. In 2023, the United Nations Special Rapporteur on contemporary forms of slavery called the temporary foreign worker program “a breeding ground for contemporary forms of slavery,” because a work permit tied to one employer leaves workers afraid to complain. Students sold fake acceptance letters by recruiters, claimants left in limbo for four years, workers competing for jobs that are not there: none of that is compassion.

It is a business model.

V. Trust

Which brings us back to this week. Ottawa did cut: the permanent target fell from 485,000 in 2024 to 380,000, and the temporary population is meant to shrink. And yet next year’s plan, all in, is up to 1.2 million people admitted, made permanent, or claiming asylum.

Again, fairly: that 1.2 million is not population growth. Many are already here, and some will leave. But the plan to shrink the temporary population assumed that many of them would go. Now, through the new pathway, many will stay, outside the number Canadians were told.

The numbers are a problem. The bigger problem is trust. Every pathway that sits off the books makes the next published target harder to believe. The old deal worked because the number Canadians saw was the number they got.

Canada can be a country of immigrants. It has been one for four hundred years, from New France and the Loyalists to the Ukrainians on the prairies and the Dutch after the war. But it has to be a country that chooses, and that tells its people the truth about what it chose.

So here is the question this essay leaves you with. If you were setting the number, what would it be, and who would you let in first? And should newcomers keep settling wherever they like, or should Canada start steering where they go?
