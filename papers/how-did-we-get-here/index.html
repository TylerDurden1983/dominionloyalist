How Did We Get Here?

The story of the Canadian economy, from the cod of the Grand Banks to the tariffs of 2026

Dominion Loyalist · November 2026

Watch the animated version on YouTube (27 minutes). The essay below follows the same path in writing.

I. Staples, and the Wood That Ruled the Waves

Canada sells three quarters of what it exports to one customer. That customer just changed the deal. To understand how we got here, we have to go back 500 years, to a fish.

From the early 1500s, Basque, Portuguese, French and English fleets fished the Grand Banks. Salted, dried cod kept for months, and it fed a Catholic Europe that ate fish on fast days. But the profits, the ships and the merchants were all in Europe. Newfoundland was a seasonal fishing station, not a place to build.

Then came fur. The felted beaver hat drove demand across Europe, and beaver underfur made the best felt. New France was built on it, and in 1670 the Crown chartered the Hudson’s Bay Company over the whole Hudson Bay drainage, Rupert’s Land. The trade only worked through First Nations partners, who trapped, guided and traded. The economist Harold Innis noticed something about it: Canada’s shape follows the fur trade’s canoe routes, east to west along rivers and lakes, not north to south like the United States.

Innis called this the staples economy. Each staple shipped a raw good out to a bigger economy and brought finished goods back. The money and the skilled work stayed overseas. Keep that pattern in mind, because it comes back in every part of this story.

The staple that mattered most was wood. Britain ruled the waves, and its navy was made of trees. HMS Victory took about 6,000 trees, 90 percent of them oak, roughly 100 acres of forest for one ship. By 1810 the Royal Navy was using about 48,000 full-grown trees a year, and an oak takes a century to mature. England had cut through its old growth. In 1802 Nelson toured the Forest of Dean and was alarmed at how little usable oak was left, and that nothing was being replanted.

The rest of Europe was in the same position. Britain’s backup was the Baltic: Riga, Danzig, Memel. Its best masts, though, came from New England white pine. Under the Broad Arrow policy of 1691, the biggest pines were reserved for the King and marked with an arrow blaze. The colonists hated it, and it became one of their grievances on the road to 1775.

Then both sources closed. The American Revolution cut off the New England masts. In 1806 and 1807, Napoleon’s Continental System shut the Baltic. Britain, at war for its survival, faced a navy it could not keep afloat.

Then, Canada. A massive, untouched forest of white and red pine, from the Saint John River to the Ottawa Valley. The Saint John valley pine had already been reserved for the Navy in 1774, and Loyalists settled New Brunswick after 1783. Between 1803 and 1811 Britain raised its duties on foreign timber, giving colonial wood a protected market. In 1806 Philemon Wright floated the first raft of squared timber from the Gatineau down the Ottawa to Quebec. Bytown, later Ottawa, grew out of this trade.

At its peak in 1845, Britain imported about 800,000 loads of Canadian timber a year. The timber ships carried immigrants west on the return voyage, so the trade populated the colonies too. It looked like enough to supply the Navy forever.

It wasn’t forever. Britain moved to free trade from 1846, and the last colonial timber preference was gone by 1860. Iron and steam replaced oak and sail, and by about 1900 the Ottawa Valley’s great pine was mostly cut. A staple is only worth what the customer wants it for, and only for as long as they want it.

II. A National Economy

In 1846 the customer walked. Peel’s government repealed the Corn Laws and slashed the timber duties, and colonial grain and wood lost their protected place in the British market. Montreal’s merchants had just poured money into canals and flour mills built for that market. The result was a depression, and in 1849 a mob burned the Parliament buildings in Montreal. The riot was over the Rebellion Losses Bill, but the anger was economic too.

That October, more than 300 Montreal businessmen signed a manifesto calling for Canada to join the United States. The list included John Redpath, two Molsons, Alexander Galt, and John Abbott, a future prime minister who later called it a youthful error. When the market disappears, loyalty gets tested. This is the first time we see it, and not the last.

In 1854 Lord Elgin negotiated a Reciprocity Treaty with the Americans: free trade in natural products like grain, timber, fish and coal. Exports south jumped, especially from southern Ontario and Nova Scotia. To be fair, part of that jump was smuggled trade becoming legal, and the American boom, first the settling of the Midwest and then the Civil War, would have pulled in goods anyway.

Meanwhile, Canada was quietly inventing the oil business. In 1846 Abraham Gesner, a Nova Scotia doctor and the son of a Loyalist officer, first showed off kerosene in Charlottetown. It was cheap, clean lamp fuel, and it went on to replace whale oil around the world. Then in 1858 James Miller Williams, a Hamilton carriage maker, dug North America’s first commercial oil well at Oil Springs, in Lambton County, Ontario. That was a year before Drake’s well in Pennsylvania, 43 years before Spindletop in Texas, and 89 years before Leduc in Alberta. By 1864 there were 27 refineries in Oil Springs and Petrolia, and Petrolia’s drillers went on to work oil fields around the world. In 1880 sixteen Ontario refiners merged into Imperial Oil, in London, Ontario, to stand up to American competition. And then? Gesner built his business in New York and died poor in Halifax in 1864. In 1898 Standard Oil bought control of Imperial. First the invention, then the company, ended up in American hands. A blessing, then a hollowing out. Hold onto that pattern.

In 1866 the Americans cancelled reciprocity. Their protectionists said only Canada was benefiting, and they resented the Province of Canada’s own tariff increases in 1858 and 1859. There was also anger over British sympathy for the Confederacy during the Civil War. Congress gave notice, and the treaty ended in March of 1866.

Having lost Britain’s preference and then the American market, the colonies had one option left: become each other’s market. Confederation in 1867 removed the tariffs between the provinces. But growth stayed slow through the long depression of the 1870s.

In 1878 John A. Macdonald won an election on a plan he called the National Policy, and in 1879 his finance minister, Leonard Tilley, rewrote nearly every line of the tariff. Average tariffs rose from about 14 percent to 21. Furniture and clocks paid 35 percent, carriages and glassware 30, boots and shoes 25, machinery 25 to 35. Raw materials stayed cheap, so factories could import their inputs and sell finished goods behind the wall. The plan had three pillars: the tariff, a railway to the Pacific, and the settlement of the West.

The last spike of the Canadian Pacific Railway was driven at Craigellachie in November 1885. The railway turned the map into an economy. Western grain could move east, and Ontario and Quebec goods could move west, all inside Canadian borders. Without it, every region would have traded north to south with its American neighbour.

Then came the wheat boom. From 1896, Clifford Sifton’s immigration drive brought settlers in by the hundreds of thousands: over 3 million arrivals between 1897 and 1914, in a country of about 5 million. A 160-acre homestead cost a $10 filing fee. Marquis wheat, developed by the federal government, ripened early enough for the short prairie season. Wheat exports went from 8 million bushels in 1896 to 75 million in 1911. Saskatchewan and Alberta became provinces in 1905, and by 1928 Canada supplied about half of the world’s wheat exports.

And the loop worked. Grain flowed east on the CPR to the port of Montreal, and Ontario and Quebec factories sold farm machinery, clothes and tools out west. Economists still debate how much the tariff helped, but a Bank of Canada study finds it was a modest net gain for the country.

It had its costs. Prairie farmers paid protected prices for their machinery and sold their wheat on the open world market, and that resentment never really went away. And American firms simply jumped the wall: Canadian General Electric in 1892, Standard Oil buying Imperial Oil in 1898, the company born from our own oil field, and Ford of Canada in 1904. The tariff built factories in Canada, but not always Canadian-owned ones.

In 1911 the country had to decide. Laurier signed a new reciprocity deal with the Americans, mostly on natural products. Manufacturers and imperial loyalists campaigned against it under the slogan “No truck nor trade with the Yankees,” and Borden’s Conservatives won. For the first time, Canada chose to build an east-west country over cheaper access to the American market, and that choice held for 77 years.

III. Workshop of the Empire

In 1897 Canada made the Empire an offer. Laurier’s government cut duties on British goods by one eighth, then by a quarter in 1898, then by a third in 1900. Canada was the first Dominion to do it, and it asked for nothing in return. It was the seed of the imperial trading bloc. Canada was trying to proudly position itself as the Empire’s senior Dominion, its loyal eldest son: its breadbasket, its timber yard, its secondary manufacturing hub, and its link across North America, with the CPR carrying the All-Red Route for mail and troops between Britain and Asia.

Within two years, the eldest son went to war. In 1899 Canada sent its first overseas contingent to South Africa. Lord Strathcona, the CPR financier, raised and paid for a mounted regiment out of his own pocket, Strathcona’s Horse, under the Mountie Sam Steele. Its effectiveness in the brutal guerrilla phase of that war caught the eye of the British high command. It was a first look at what kind of son the Empire had.

In the Great War, the Canadian Corps earned its name as the Empire’s shock troops. At first the Empire was taken aback by its eldest son’s brutality, and his seeming appetite for war. At Christmas 1915, a year after the famous truce, Canadians near Ypres answered German cigars and holiday greetings with rifle fire. They made the night trench raid their trademark, and British writers who saw them, like the poet Robert Graves and the war correspondent Philip Gibbs, remarked on how ruthlessly they fought, including a reputation for taking few prisoners. But the Empire came to see the advantage of an eldest son raised by wolves and bears in the forests of the North. By 1918 the Canadian Corps was the spearhead, sent wherever the hardest blow had to land. And the country behind them had to arm them. At first, it couldn’t.

Canada entered the war with almost no munitions industry. Sam Hughes’s Shell Committee handed contracts to friends, some of whom had no factories, and the shells arrived late, many of them duds. In November 1915 Britain set up the Imperial Munitions Board, and put a Toronto businessman, Joseph Flavelle, in charge. He cleared out the profiteers, took over the failing firms, and hired women into the plants. By 1917 more than 600 factories with 250,000 workers were making close to 100,000 shells a day, between a quarter and a third of all the ammunition British artillery fired in France. By the war’s end: about 65 million shells, 2,900 aircraft, 88 ships, and Canada’s first chemical industry. The son at the front and the factory at home grew up together.

It came at a cost. The work was dangerous: late in the war, the British Chemical Company plant at Trenton, Ontario, exploded, and the whole town had to evacuate. It was concentrated, too: about 60 percent of the board’s output came from Ontario, which the West resented. And when the war ended, the board shut down and the plants went back to civilian goods.

Then the Depression. World trade collapsed, and every country raised its tariffs. In the summer of 1932, R.B. Bennett, a New Brunswicker and a committed imperialist, hosted the British Empire Economic Conference in the House of Commons. The rule they agreed on was simple: home producers first, Empire producers second, foreigners last. It produced a set of five-year deals. It did not cure the Depression, but it locked in the imperial trading bloc for the decade. For a few weeks, the whole family sat at the eldest son’s table, in his own Parliament.

On the eve of the next war, in 1938, Britain still took about 40 percent of Canada’s exports, and the United States about a third. Hold onto those numbers, because in twenty years they flip.

When war came again, the eldest son answered the call, but this time by his own Parliament’s vote, declaring war on September 10, 1939, a week after Britain. C.D. Howe, an American-born engineer who had built grain elevators, ran the Department of Munitions and Supply, and became known as the minister of everything. He recruited executives at a dollar a year and created 28 Crown corporations where no industry existed: Polymer for synthetic rubber, Victory Aircraft for Lancaster bombers, Research Enterprises for radar and optics. The economy grew from about 5 billion dollars to 12, with 1.1 million Canadians working on war production. Output included about 815,000 military vehicles, and 70 percent of everything went to the Allies. Manufacturing reached more than a quarter of the economy, the highest in our history.

Britain came out of the war broke and short of dollars, and could no longer buy the way it used to. Howe sold most of the Crown corporations to private owners. American money poured in instead: by the 1960s, foreign firms, mostly American, controlled nearly 60 percent of Canadian manufacturing. Branch plants made American designs for the Canadian market, and did their research back in the States. In 1957 Diefenbaker proposed shifting 15 percent of Canada’s imports from the United States to Britain. London countered with full free trade, Ottawa backed away, and nothing came of it.

The son got left behind. The proud eldest son had bled for the Empire in three wars, South Africa and both world wars, and now the family estate was breaking up around him. India went in 1947. At Suez in 1956, Canada even sided against Britain’s last attempts at imperial influence. And the new lord of the Anglosphere was the rebel cousin who left the family in the first place, British by birth but remade in the melting pot: the United States. The eldest son was left somewhat directionless.

The Avro Arrow shows where that left us. The CF-105 was a world-class Mach 2 interceptor, built in Malton, Ontario. It was also staggeringly expensive. The program’s cost is usually given as 1.1 billion dollars, about 12 billion in today’s money. Against an economy of roughly 34 billion dollars in 1959, that is over 3 percent of everything Canada produced, the same share as about 100 billion dollars today. Cabinet was told a full production run would cost about 2 billion over five years. The price per plane had climbed from about 2 million dollars to about 12 million. And there was no export market: the Arrow was built around Canada’s own needs, and the Americans and the British would not buy it. A small country cannot carry a program that size for one customer, itself. It was the eldest son’s attempt to stand on his own, and the bill was too big to carry alone. On February 20, 1959, Diefenbaker cancelled it, and about 14,000 Avro workers were laid off that day. The bitter part is what came next. Every airframe and all the tooling were cut up, and dozens of Avro’s engineers went to NASA to work on Mercury, Gemini and Apollo. The cancellation was defensible. The way we threw the capability away was not.

For about 60 years, from 1897 to the late 1950s, the Empire gave its eldest son a market and a reason to build. When the family estate broke up, nothing Canadian replaced it. The neighbour did.

IV. Drifting South

With the Empire gone, the eldest son tied his fortunes to the old rebel next door. It made us richer, and it also made us dependent.

There was one deal that got it right. Before 1965, Canada ran a 785 million dollar auto trade deficit with the United States, and small Canadian plants built too many models at too high a cost. The Auto Pact removed tariffs on cars and parts, but only for automakers that kept building in Canada. Production here had to keep pace with sales here, with a minimum of Canadian value added. Canadian-built vehicles exported to the US went from 48,000 in 1965 to 849,000 in 1975. Canada’s share of North American output rose from 7.1 percent to 11.2 by 1971, and by 1970 the deficit had become a surplus. It was open trade with a guarantee attached. A few people with foresight negotiated for our share, instead of hoping for it.

Elsewhere, though, the branch plants kept spreading, and by the late 1960s Ottawa had a name for the problem. The 1968 Watkins Report opened by saying that foreign control of Canadian industry was unique among industrialized nations, and the 1972 Gray Report put it at nearly 60 percent of manufacturing. Branch plants rarely did research here, bought their parts from the parent company’s suppliers, and made their big decisions abroad. Ottawa responded with the Foreign Investment Review Agency in 1974, which the Mulroney government later abolished.

On January 1, 1973, Britain joined the European Economic Community, and the last of the old imperial preferences went with it. The parent had joined another family. Canadian farm exports to Britain lost ground almost at once. Trudeau’s government saw it coming. In 1972 Mitchell Sharp laid out three options: carry on as we were, integrate more closely with the United States, or take a Third Option and diversify toward Europe and Japan. Ottawa chose the third. It failed. Dependence on the United States kept rising through the 1970s and 80s.

In January 1988 Brian Mulroney and Ronald Reagan signed a free trade agreement. John Turner forced an election on it, and in the debate he attacked the deal as a sellout of Canadian sovereignty. On November 21 the Progressive Conservatives won a majority of 169 seats, even though most votes went to the parties opposing the deal. The agreement took effect on January 1, 1989, and NAFTA added Mexico in 1994. It was the reverse of 1911. This time Canada chose the American market, and the eldest son moved his business in with the rebel.

So what did free trade actually do? Trade and output grew. Through the 1990s, manufacturing’s share of the economy actually rose, from 1994 to 2001, helped by a cheap dollar near 65 cents US. But the industry reorganized to fit the continent. Plants specialized in one part of a US-run supply chain, with the head offices and the decisions south of the border. We got richer and more tied in at the same time.

Then, in 2001, three blows landed in one year. In February the Auto Pact formally ended, after the World Trade Organization ruled its guarantees illegal on complaints from Japan and Europe. In December, China joined the WTO and became the world’s factory. And the commodity boom began.

Oil, gas and mineral prices soared, and the loonie rose about 60 percent from 2002, passing par with the US dollar in 2007. For our factories, that worked like a price cut for every foreign competitor. From 2004 to 2008, more than one in seven manufacturing jobs disappeared, and auto parts lost over a quarter of its workforce. In all, roughly half a million manufacturing jobs went in the 2000s, while the oil sands and the mines boomed. Economists call this pattern Dutch disease.

To be fair, Statistics Canada finds that much of manufacturing’s shrinking share comes from manufactured goods getting cheaper relative to everything else, through productivity, not just from making less. Every rich country’s manufacturing share fell. But Canada’s real manufacturing output also fell behind after 2000, and the jobs did not come back. By 2024 manufacturing was about 10 percent of our economy, against 19 percent in Germany.

Not all the wounds came from outside. We sit on the world’s third-largest proven oil reserves, and we could not get that oil to any coast but the American one. Northern Gateway to the Pacific was approved, then struck down in court, then cancelled by Ottawa in 2016. Energy East to Saint John was cancelled by its builder in 2017. Trans Mountain: Ottawa bought it for 4.5 billion dollars in 2018, and the expansion ended up costing about 34 billion, more than six times the 2013 estimate. Meanwhile, the provinces still trade with each other through walls of their own rules. In January 2026 the IMF estimated that our internal trade barriers act like a 9 percent tariff, bigger than the average American tariff on Canada, and that removing them could add about 7 percent to the economy, roughly 210 billion dollars. Each decision had its arguments: Indigenous consultation, the environment, provincial jurisdiction. Add them up, and the result is a country that sells its oil to one buyer at a discount, and still struggles to sell its own wine and beer across provincial lines. Nobody in Washington did this to us.

And the people leave too. This is the oldest hollowing out of all. In the 50 years before 1900, about 1.8 million Canadians moved to the United States, and between 1840 and 1930 roughly 900,000 French Canadians left Quebec for the mill towns of New England. Between 1861 and 1901 Canada barely grew, because people left about as fast as they arrived. It hasn’t stopped. About 830,000 Canadian-born people live in the US today, and millions more Americans have Canadian roots. The ones who leave are disproportionately the builders. A 2018 study found that in some top programs, up to two thirds of software engineering graduates were working abroad, mostly in the States. Canadians co-founded Uber, Slack and OpenAI, in the United States. In 2016 about three quarters of Canadian founders who had raised over a million dollars were based in Canada. By 2024 only about a third were, and 88 percent of those who left had been educated at Canadian universities. The spirit is here, and it always has been. We train the builders, and they build somewhere else.

Why a country this rich in land, resources and talent keeps getting in its own way is a bigger question than this video can answer. It goes back to how Canada was founded, and why we turned out so different from the Americans.

So where does that leave us? In 2024 about 76 percent of Canada’s exports went to one country. Crude oil and gas alone were about a fifth of all our goods exports in 2025, along with cars, metals, lumber and grain. Raw goods to a bigger partner, finished goods back. It is the staples economy again, with a different customer.

And in 2025 the bill came due. The United States put tariffs on Canadian steel, aluminum, autos and lumber. On July 1, 2026, at its first scheduled review, the US declined to renew our trade agreement, which stays in force, but now under annual review. On August 22, 2026, new 50 percent tariffs hit about 20 billion dollars a year of Canadian goods, trade deal or no trade deal. Canada has started looking elsewhere: the share of our exports going to the United States fell from 76 percent in 2024 to 72 in 2025.

V. The Pattern

Look back at the pattern. The pine forest. The oil at Oil Springs. The shell factories. Howe’s Crown corporations. The Arrow. The Auto Pact. Each one a blessing, and each one hollowed out, some from outside and some from within. Canada could quite easily be an unfathomably rich country. It has the land, the water, the energy, the minerals and the people. What it keeps doing is shooting itself in the foot.

Each one a blessing, and each one hollowed out, some from outside and some from within.

But we have been here before. In 1846 Britain walked away, and we built a country. In 1866 the Americans cancelled reciprocity, and we answered with Confederation. Losing a customer has been the start of our best chapters, whenever we chose to build. The eldest son has no parent left to build for. The question is whether he will build for himself.
