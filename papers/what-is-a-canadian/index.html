What Is a Canadian?

A pin in Manchester, a piece of string, and fifteen hundred years of blending

Dominion Loyalist · October 2026

Watch the animated version on YouTube (12 minutes). The essay below follows the same path in writing.

I. Way, Way Back

What is a Canadian? It sounds like an easy question. Ask a hundred people and you'll get a hundred answers: hockey, apologetic politeness, not being American. Those are habits, though, not an identity. To explain what a Canadian actually is, where we came from and why we turned out the way we did, you have to go a long way back. To northern Europe, and the British Isles.

Around the year 450, the coastline running from the Netherlands up into Denmark was home to a handful of related Germanic peoples. Three of them matter here. In the north, on the Jutland peninsula, the Jutes. Just below them, in a small district still called Angeln today, the Angles. And to the south, between the Elbe and the Weser, the Saxons. They spoke closely related languages. They farmed, fished and fought. And they looked west, across the North Sea, at an island that had just been abandoned by its rulers.

Rome had held Britain for almost four hundred years. By around 410 the legions were gone, and the Romano-British were left to fend for themselves. Before long the North Sea peoples were crossing over, some as hired soldiers and many more as settlers. The Jutes took Kent. The Saxons spread along the south coast and the Thames valley. The Angles took the east and the north, as far as the Firth of Forth. Over the following centuries the land took their name: Engla land, the land of the Angles. England.

It's tempting to picture this as one people wiping out another. The genetics say otherwise. A large study of the British population published in 2015 found that in central and southern England, Anglo-Saxon ancestry makes up somewhere between ten and forty percent. The newcomers didn't replace the Britons. They married into them.

That is the first thing to understand about where Canadians come from. It isn't the story of one tribe. It's a story of blending.

II. Cousins Across the Channel

While the Angles and Saxons were moving into England, something was moving the other way. Britons from the southwest crossed the Channel to the Armorican peninsula, in what is now France. So many came that the region took their name too: Brittany, Little Britain. Breton is still a close cousin of Welsh and Cornish.

The Channel was never really a wall. It was a watery road, and it had been one for a very long time. The same 2015 study found evidence of a large migration across the Channel into Britain well before the Romans arrived. Long before anyone was English or French, the people on both sides of that water were already related.

Then came the Northmen. In the late 700s, Scandinavian raiders, later known as Vikings, began hitting the coasts of Britain and France. By the 800s, Danish armies had carved out a large part of eastern and northern England, the region that came to be called the Danelaw. Place names ending in -by and -thorpe, like Derby, Grimsby and Scunthorpe, are their fingerprints. So are everyday English words like sky, egg and knife. The Vikings were raiding the towns of their ancient Angle and Saxon cousins, likely with no idea how close the kinship really was.

On the other side of the Channel, another band of Northmen pushed up the Seine. In 911 the French king made a deal with their leader, Rollo: he would get the lower Seine valley, and in return he would defend it against other raiders. His people became the Northmen of France, the Normans, and their land became Normandy. Within a few generations they spoke French, worshipped as French Christians and had married into the local population. Norse by origin, French by culture.

In 1066 the Duke of Normandy crossed the Channel and took the Crown of England. Consider what that means. The English were a blend of Britons, Angles, Saxons, Jutes and Danes. Their new ruling class was Norman: Norse settlers who had blended into northern France. The two sides of the Channel now shared a king, a nobility and, for a few centuries, even a language at court.

So when we hear about the "two solitudes" of English and French Canada, it's worth remembering that the ancestors of both had been crossing that narrow strip of water, trading, raiding and marrying, for thousands of years.

English-speaking Canada and French-speaking Canada are blood-cousins, not just neighbours.

III. The String on Manchester

Here is a simple way to see it. Take a map of Europe and put a pin in Manchester, roughly the middle of the British Isles. Tie a string to it, about 850 kilometres long, and draw a circle. Inside that circle sit all of England, Scotland, Wales and Ireland; Normandy, Brittany and Paris; the Atlantic coast of France down past La Rochelle; the Low Countries; and the old homelands of the Angles and the Jutes. Now look at who left that circle for Canada.

Start with New France. Between the 1600s and 1763, only about ten thousand French settlers put down permanent roots in the St. Lawrence valley. Almost every French Canadian alive today descends from that small founding group. They came mostly from the northwest and the Atlantic coast. Normandy and the Paris region were the two biggest sources, followed by Poitou, Aunis and Saintonge (the country around La Rochelle), then Brittany. All inside the circle. Many Québécois families trace back to the same Normandy that produced the conquerors of England, or to lands that were once part of the Anglo-French Angevin Empire.

The French weren't alone in North America. Down the Atlantic coast the British had built colonies of their own, and they were growing fast. By the time of the final showdown, New France had around 70,000 people; the British colonies had well over a million. For a century and a half the two empires fought over the continent, usually as part of larger wars in Europe, and usually with First Nations allies on both sides.

In 1713 France gave up Acadia, Newfoundland and the Hudson Bay lands to Britain, and Acadia became Nova Scotia. The final round began in the 1750s in the Ohio Valley and grew into the Seven Years' War. In 1755 the British began deporting the Acadians from Nova Scotia. More than ten thousand were forced out and scattered across the British colonies, France and eventually Louisiana, where their descendants became the Cajuns. In 1758 the fortress of Louisbourg fell. In September 1759, on the Plains of Abraham outside Quebec City, a British army under James Wolfe defeated the French under the Marquis de Montcalm in a battle that lasted less than an hour. Both commanders were mortally wounded, one dying in victory, the other in valiant defeat. Montreal surrendered the next year, and in 1763 the Treaty of Paris made it official. France kept only Saint-Pierre and Miquelon, two small islands off Newfoundland that are still French today.

Here is the part that matters. The Canadiens didn't leave, and unlike the Acadians, they weren't forced out. They stayed on their farms along the St. Lawrence, and in 1774 the Quebec Act let them keep their Catholic faith, their French civil law and their landholding system, all under the British Crown. When the American colonies rebelled and invaded Quebec in 1775, the vast majority of Canadiens refused to join them. By the 1770s, two peoples from opposite sides of the English Channel were living under one Crown again, as they had after 1066. A new Angevin Empire, this time in North America.

Then the British came. First the Loyalists, tens of thousands of them, who fled the American Revolution in the 1780s to stay under the Crown. Then the Scots, many pushed out of the Highlands, themselves a blend of Gaels, Picts, Britons, Angles and Norse. Then the Irish, especially during and after the famine of the 1840s, from an Ireland where the Vikings had founded Dublin, Waterford and Cork. And a steady flow of English the whole way through.

The census of 1871 gives a snapshot of the four original provinces at Confederation. About 31 percent of Canadians reported French origin, 24 percent Irish, 20 percent English and 16 percent Scottish. More than nine in ten traced their roots to the British Isles or France. Add the smaller numbers of Germans and Dutch and you reach roughly 98 percent. Nearly all of it from inside that one circle around the North Sea and the English Channel.

IV. A New People

So is a Canadian just a transplanted Brit, or a Frenchman in a colder climate? No, and this is the interesting part. When people from that circle crossed the Atlantic, they didn't stay what they were. Normans, Bretons and Parisians became Canadiens within a couple of generations, with their own accent, their own habits and their own sense of being from here rather than from France. English and Irish Loyalists from New York and Scots from the Highlands ended up neighbours in Upper Canada and became something neither had been before.

They were also shaped by the people who were already here. The First Nations were the guides, trading partners and allies without whom New France and British North America would not have survived. On the prairies, French and Scottish fur traders married into Cree and Ojibwe families, and a new nation was born from it: the Métis.

That process has a name: ethnogenesis, the birth of a new people. A nation isn't just a country; it's a people, and in Canada a new one was born. It is exactly what happened in England fifteen hundred years ago, when Angles, Saxons, Jutes and Britons blended into the English. It happened again here.

What came out the other side was a people with a shared inheritance. The Crown. Parliament. The English common law in most of the country and French civil law in Quebec. Two languages that had been borrowing from each other since 1066. Institutions built over a thousand years around the same narrow stretch of sea.

None of that is a membership test. The 1871 census already counted thousands of Black Canadians, mostly in Nova Scotia and Ontario, descended from Black Loyalists and from freedom-seekers who came north on the Underground Railroad seeking the protection of the Crown. Since then, millions of people from every corner of the world have come here wanting to become Canadian, the same way Rollo's Northmen became French and the Angles became English. That is how this has always worked.

Large modern waves of immigration have made the question "what is a Canadian?" somewhat contentious. It shouldn't be. The way to keep it working is to tell the story truthfully, without shame and with some pride. Anyone can become Canadian in time, but Canadian is a real nationality, not simply a piece of paper awarded by the government. It deserves to be celebrated, and never to the exclusion of others who wish to become Canadian too.

If you want to understand why Canada has a King, a Parliament, two official languages, a strange reverence for the intangible thing called "the Crown," and a deep suspicion of making a fuss, you have to start where we did: with a pin in Manchester, a piece of string, and a few boatloads of Angles crossing the North Sea.
