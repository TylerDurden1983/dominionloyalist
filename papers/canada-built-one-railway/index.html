Canada Built One Railway. Then Gave Up.

We built a railway across a continent in under five years. Then we stopped building trunks. What it would take to open the rest of the country: corridors, with rail at the core.

Dominion Loyalist · November 2026

Watch the animated version on YouTube (18 minutes). The essay below follows the same path in writing.

On November 7, 1885, at a place called Craigellachie in the mountains of British Columbia, one of the railway’s backers drove an iron spike into a tie. With that, Canada was joined coast to coast. Ottawa had given the Canadian Pacific ten years to finish the line. It was done in under five.

Now look at the map today. More than a century later, the main lines across this country are still mostly a single set of tracks, with sidings where trains pull over and wait for each other to pass. Two out of three Canadians live within a hundred kilometres of the American border. Most of this country has never had a way in.

We built one trunk line in record time. Then we stopped building trunks.

This essay looks at how that happened, why Canada struggles to build anything big, and what a real national plan could look like. Not just a railway. A corridor.

I. One Trunk, Then Spurs

British Columbia joined Confederation in 1871 on one promise: a railway to the east, started within two years and finished within ten. Ottawa missed that deadline. The Pacific Scandal of 1873 brought down Macdonald’s government, and the line stalled.

In 1881 a new syndicate took over, with twenty-five million dollars in cash and twenty-five million acres of land. They blasted across the Canadian Shield, bridged the Rockies, and drove the last spike in 1885. One line, coast to coast, built to hold a country together.

Then came the wheat boom. Settlers poured onto the Prairies, and everyone wanted a piece of the traffic. Two more companies set out to build their own transcontinentals: the Canadian Northern, and the Grand Trunk Pacific with its line to Prince Rupert. Between 1900 and 1915, Canada’s railway mileage doubled, from about seventeen and a half thousand miles to nearly thirty-five thousand.

That sounds like nation-building. It wasn’t. Three companies were racing each other across the same wheat fields, and many of the new lines were placed to hurt a competitor, not to open new country. Between Edmonton and Calgary, two of them ran separate branch lines side by side, each with its own string of little towns. This was not a network of trunks. It was a tangle of spurs.

Then the war came, money dried up, and both newcomers went bankrupt. Between 1918 and 1923, Ottawa took them over and folded them into Canadian National. CN inherited a system of lines that had been built to compete with each other.

There was one last big push. In 1929, a crew of three thousand men, working with pickaxes and wheelbarrows on frozen muskeg, finished the Hudson Bay Railway to Churchill. Prairie farmers finally had a rail line to salt water.

And after that, Ottawa drew the wrong lesson. Not “plan better.” Just “build less.” In the 1970s, two federal studies recommended pulling up over three thousand miles of prairie branch lines. By 2005, about 6,600 miles had been abandoned: thirty-five percent of the peak.

II. The Nation That Stopped Building

The Alaska Highway

Ask a Canadian about building the North, and sooner or later someone mentions the Alaska Highway. We’re proud of it. We put it on postcards. We didn’t build it. The United States Army did, in about eight months in 1942, after Pearl Harbor. Canada supplied the land and waived the customs duties. The Americans did the work and paid for it.

Washington even offered to hand the road over before the war ended. Ottawa said no, because of the cost of repairing and maintaining it. Canada finally took it over in April 1946. After the war, we paid the Americans back for the airfields, buildings and phone lines along the route. Not for the road itself. The most famous road in our north was built by a foreign army in a hurry.

Mid-Canada and the Mackenzie Valley

Twenty-five years later, someone tried to change that. Richard Rohmer was a Second World War fighter pilot, and later a lawyer and a major general. In 1967 he proposed the Mid-Canada Development Corridor: a belt of railways, roads and new cities across the boreal north. His price tag was up to five billion dollars over twenty years, about forty-five billion today. He took it to Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau, who never backed it, and nothing came of it.

In the same years, Ottawa drew up guidelines for a pipeline corridor down the Mackenzie Valley, from the Arctic coast to Alberta. In 1977, the Berger Inquiry found a pipeline was feasible, but recommended waiting ten years to settle Indigenous land claims first. Ten years. In Canada. Those claims ran through the 1980s and well into the 1990s. The government agreed to wait, and that was fair. But when a second attempt finally came, it was cancelled in 2017. Fifty years, and no pipe.

What we kept

So what did we keep? The same thin lines. On CN’s busiest main line, Edmonton to Winnipeg, less than 150 of 800 miles was double-track as of 2019. CN is adding second track a few miles at a time.

And passengers pay for it. VIA Rail owns only about three percent of the track it runs on. The rest belongs to the freight railways, and nothing in Canadian law puts passenger trains first. In early 2025, VIA’s on-time performance fell to thirty percent. On the short stretch of track VIA actually owns, between Ottawa and Montreal, it runs on time about ninety percent of the time.

Ottawa’s answer is Alto: a thousand kilometres of new high-speed passenger line from Toronto to Quebec City. Alto says $60 billion to $90 billion. The Parliamentary Budget Officer says $75 billion to $113 billion, just to build it. Quebec’s new separatist government already opposes it.

We think that’s backwards. A passenger-only line in a country of forty million will struggle to ever pay for itself. It is always competing with faster flights, and with Canadians who think nothing of a five-hour drive.

So why do Europe and Asia keep building while we don’t? Part of it is density: they have cities at both ends and towns all along the way. But part of it is planning. Their governments plan networks. We approve projects one at a time: a mine road here, a single power line there, each one studied, fought over and consulted on separately, from scratch. We keep building spurs, and we keep paying for every one of them in full.

III. The Corridor

Here’s what we’d build instead. Not a road. Not a pipeline. A corridor.

One strip of land, set aside once, studied once, negotiated once. Rail is the foundation, double track from day one. Beside it runs a highway. Then power lines. Then pipelines, for oil and gas, and water for the towns along the way. Then fibre. Whatever the next generation needs, there’s already room for it.

And we’d build it for freight first. Not high-speed trains: fast, reliable, high-capacity freight. With two tracks, trains stop waiting in sidings for each other, and average speeds go up. Freight pays the bills. Passenger service can ride on that later, on track the corridor already has room for.

This isn’t 1910 again. Back then, three companies laid competing lines through the same farmland, chasing the same wheat. This is the opposite: one shared trunk per region, planned once, running to places where the customers are already waiting. Mines with no way out. Ports with no rail behind them. Towns paying for diesel.

Who pays

A public corridor authority owns the strip, the way a city owns its streets. Pipeline, power and fibre companies rent space in it. CN, CPKC and VIA pay to run on the track. And because the public owns that track, passenger trains finally get a fair shot at it, instead of waiting in a siding for a grain train.

Ottawa already has the power

Railways that cross provincial lines are federal. On top of that, the constitution lets Parliament declare any work to be for the general advantage of Canada, which brings it under federal control even if it sits inside one province. Parliament used that power hundreds of times, mostly for railways, then stopped after 1961. This year it dusted it off for Alto. The provinces still own what’s in the ground, but the corridor itself can be built as one national work. And the new Building Canada Act lets Ottawa fast-track projects it lists as being in the national interest. The Mackenzie Valley Highway has already been put forward for that list.

This isn’t one group’s fantasy. Since 2015, the University of Calgary’s School of Public Policy has studied a seven-thousand-kilometre corridor of road, rail, pipeline, power and communications across the country. Ottawa’s new Major Projects Office now talks about corridors too: an upgraded rail line and a pipeline to Churchill, and a road to a new Arctic port at Grays Bay.

But look at the Ring of Fire, one of the richest mineral deposits in the country. Ontario is building roads to it, opening around 2030. Roads only. No rail, no shared right-of-way. That’s another spur.

And there’s a reason to do it now. In 2025, about eighty-seven percent of our crude oil exports went to one customer: the United States, the same country putting tariffs on us. Most of it leaves through pipelines that end in America. Corridors give us our own roads to salt water.

IV. Six Corridors

The Pacific corridor starts at the port of Prince Rupert, runs inland to Terrace, then north along Highway 37 through Stewart, Iskut and Dease Lake to Watson Lake in the Yukon. This is BC’s Golden Triangle: copper and gold at Red Chris and Brucejack today, and giant deposits like KSM and Galore Creek still waiting. Right now, the road into Brucejack ends with twelve kilometres across a glacier, and the one power line up there, finished in 2014, is already running short of capacity.

The Prairie corridor runs north from Edmonton on track that already exists, to Hay River on Great Slave Lake. Ottawa built that line in 1964, under Diefenbaker’s “Roads to Resources,” and finished it eighteen months ahead of schedule. It was the last time we did this. From Hay River, the corridor rounds the lake to Yellowknife and the Slave Geological Province: diamond mines that carried the territory for a generation and are now closing, and what comes after them, Canada’s first rare earth mine at Nechalacho and the NICO cobalt project. Then it heads down the Mackenzie Valley, through Norman Wells and Inuvik, to Tuktoyaktuk. Not for a navy, since it’s mostly too shallow, but for the Mackenzie Delta, where about six trillion cubic feet of gas has sat in three proven fields since the early 1970s.

The Arctic corridor comes later. From the Delta, it runs east along the mainland coast toward Kugluktuk and Cambridge Bay, tying into the new port at Grays Bay. That opens the Beaufort’s offshore gas, and a Canadian land link along the Northwest Passage.

The Manitoba corridor follows the Hudson Bay Railway from Winnipeg through The Pas, Thompson and Gillam to Churchill, rebuilt and double-tracked, with a pipeline from Alberta alongside it. Yes, Churchill’s port is only open about four months a year today. But the ice-free window has grown by about a month since the 1980s, and new studies say purpose-built ships could reach it nine or ten months a year. Then it keeps going, up the coast to Arviat, Whale Cove, Rankin Inlet and Chesterfield Inlet, with a spur to Baker Lake. Every one of those communities makes its electricity from diesel. An Inuit-owned company, Nukik, is already planning a twelve-hundred-kilometre power and fibre line along almost exactly this route, to those five communities and two gold mines. That’s half a corridor. Put the rail and the road beside it.

The Ontario corridor runs north from the existing railhead at Moosonee, up the James Bay coast through Fort Albany, Kashechewan and Attawapiskat, then west into the Ring of Fire: chromite for stainless steel, plus nickel, copper and platinum. The First Nations closest to it, like Webequie and Neskantaga, can only be reached by air or winter road, and they still run on diesel. From there, the corridor carries on across the Hudson Bay Lowlands through Peawanuck to Churchill, joining Ontario to Manitoba’s port.

The Quebec corridor runs north from Montreal through Chibougamau and Matagami into Eeyou Istchee, where the Cree and Quebec have already floated a rail line up the James Bay highway, and where lithium mines like Whabouchi are opening. Then east to the Labrador Trough, some of the highest-grade iron ore on earth, around Schefferville, Labrador City and Wabush. That’s next to Churchill Falls and Gull Island, where Ottawa, Quebec and Newfoundland signed a ten-billion-dollar power deal this August. From there it runs on through Goose Bay to the Strait of Belle Isle and Newfoundland.

We wouldn’t build all six at once. Start where half the work is done. In Manitoba, track already reaches Churchill and the Kivalliq power line is already being planned. On the Prairies, track already reaches Hay River. Prove it there. Then go north, and east.

V. Can We Still Build?

So that’s the plan. Six corridors, with rail at the core and power, pipe and fibre running beside it, and freight paying the way. Towns that run on diesel get the grid. Mines reached by ice road and helicopter get a railway. And Canada gets a second spine, north of the first.

The costs

Will this be cheap? No. The ground is hard, the winters are long, and every kilometre crosses someone’s land. Most of these routes run through First Nations and Inuit territory. The answer there is ownership, not just consultation: a real share of the corridor, and of everything that moves through it. Everything worth doing is difficult.

And yes, there’s an environmental cost. The Hudson Bay Lowlands are the second-largest peatland on earth. The Bathurst caribou herd, whose range lies across the route to Grays Bay, has collapsed from about four hundred and seventy thousand animals in the 1980s to about thirty-six hundred today. That’s real, and corridors have to be routed around calving grounds and migration paths. But one shared corridor cuts the land once. Six separate roads, power lines and pipelines, each fought over one at a time, cut it six times.

Muskeg

Before anyone says it: yes, you can build on muskeg. It’s just not easy. The Hudson Bay Railway has crossed it all the way to Churchill since 1929.

There’s more than one way to do it. On peat, engineers can float the embankment instead of digging the bog out: a thick layer of gravel over a geotextile mat that spreads the load. Where there’s permafrost, you build in winter and only add fill, never cut, so the ground underneath stays frozen. That’s how the Inuvik to Tuktoyaktuk Highway was built, and it opened in 2017. China ran a railway across five hundred and fifty kilometres of permafrost on the Tibetan Plateau, using crushed-rock embankments that pull cold air into the ground, and heat pipes that keep it frozen.

Where the bog is too deep, or the ground too warm, you stop fighting it. You drive piles down to solid footing and run the line on a low bridge. China did exactly that on stretches of its Tibet railway. The Trans-Alaska Pipeline rides above the permafrost on seventy-eight thousand steel supports. It costs more per kilometre, so you save it for the worst ground. But it works.

What muskeg really costs is upkeep. The Hudson Bay line sinks a little every summer as the permafrost under it thaws. In 2017, floods and collapsing ground cut it for about a year and a half. That’s not an argument against building. It’s an argument for building it properly the first time.

Who builds it

The same kind of force that built the Alaska Highway: an army. Ours. Give the Canadian Armed Forces a standing mission to build these corridors, alongside civilian contractors.

We’ve done a version of this before. From 1946 to 1964, the Royal Canadian Engineers ran and maintained the Alaska Highway for eighteen years. China built much of its rail network with a railway corps inside its army, then turned it into a civilian construction giant.

Think of what that buys. Soldiers who train every year on permafrost, muskeg, bridges and rail, in our own north. Real presence across the Arctic, not just on paper. And an engineering force with more hard-country building experience than almost any ally has: the one our allies call when they need something built.

Rohmer priced his whole plan at about forty-five billion in today’s money. The real bill would be higher now. But that’s the scale we’re already willing to spend on one passenger line between Toronto and Quebec City.

In 1885, we built a line across a country that barely existed, in under five years. The question isn’t whether Canada can build. It’s whether we still want to.
